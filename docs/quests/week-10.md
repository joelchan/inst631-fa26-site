## 🃏 PLAY

**Unplug the mouse.**

This is **hour one of your [Accessibility Testing Mission](../missions/accessibility-testing.md)**: so keep the play outputs and add to your mission!

Before you start, decide on:

- **The page** you're going to evaluate.
- **The task** — the thing a person actually comes to that page to do. Buy the thing, book the appointment, find the opening hours, submit the form. Use this same task for the mission's screen reader pass, so the two hours are comparable.

Now put your mouse and trackpad somewhere you can't reach them.

1. **~10 min — get oriented.** Tab, Shift-Tab, Enter, Space, arrow keys. That's your whole vocabulary now: no more mouse!
2. **~40 min — try to do the task.** Keep a running list of every time you:
	- **lost the focus ring** and couldn't tell where you were
	- **couldn't reach something** you could plainly see
	- **got trapped** somewhere you couldn't Tab out of
	- **landed somewhere surprising** — focus jumping somewhere that made no sense
	- **had to Tab through forty things** to get to the one you wanted
3. **~10 min — post your list.** However rough. Note where you wanted to give up — rage is data!

!!! note "This is not a disability simulation!"

    An hour of doing this doesn't tell you what anyone's life is like. But it's useful as an **instrument**, the same reason the screen reader is in the mission. A great deal of assistive technology (switches, sip-and-puff, head wands, eye tracking, voice control) either works through the keyboard or emulates it. A page you can't drive by keyboard can't be driven by most of them either, so it's useful to uncover how things break when you don't have a mouse!

    Keep your findings about the **page**.


!!! tip "Why keyboard first, and *then* the screen reader?"

    They're **two instruments with different sensitivities**, not the same test run twice.

    **Some failures can only be seen.** A screen reader announces what has focus whether or not anything is drawn on screen — so a missing focus ring (WCAG **2.4.7 Focus Visible**), focus landing behind a sticky header (**2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured**), or an indicator too faint to find (**2.4.13 Focus Appearance**) are all invisible to the screen reader pass and obvious in this one. So this play will complement what you'll do with the screen reader.

    **And one new thing at a time.** Using a screen reader well requires learning how to use the navigation layer, the speech layer, browse-vs-focus modes and verbosity settings, all at once. That's a lot! So this play introduces you to navigation first, while you can still *see* where focus is, so that speech is the only new variable later — and if something is baffling in hour two, you'll know whether it's you or the page.

    Worth knowing: **practitioners don't usually split these.** An experienced evaluatoroften runs the screen reader *with* the keyboard, because that's closer to a real workflow. We're splitting them so you can feel what each one is good at.


## ✍️ Reflection

Three short paragraphs — **250–500 words total**.

**Snag.** One specific place you got stuck, reread, or bounced off. Quote the sentence, or give the page number or timestamp. If you feel inspired, try to articulate what the snag is, but even the quote/sentence/timestamp alone is fine. *"I didn't really get ID 1"* is too vague to be a snag; better would be something like *"I couldn't understand Norman's distinction between real and perceived affordances (ID 1, p. 30)"*.

**Compare.** Reflect on your PLAY in relation to the READ or WATCH, and try to identify a *"huh. that's funny/interesting/weird/cool"* or *"ahhh i see/get it now"* reaction that connects one idea (e.g., a concept, a design heuristic, or something else) with something concrete in your PLAY (process or product).

**Ask.** One question you *cannot* answer from this week's materials and think is worth class time.
